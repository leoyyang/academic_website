abstract: Why might public reporting matter for officials' careers when local failures are already documented in official records? This paper examines this question in China, where provincial authorities oversee the local press and appoint prefecture leaders. I link official coal-mine accident records, newspaper reports, and prefecture party secretaries' career histories for 2003–2012. The records provide a common measure of accident severity and include accidents without matched reporting. Conditional on fatalities and other observed characteristics, greater non-local coverage is associated with less favorable career outcomes. I interpret this relationship through the incentives of provincial leaders, who evaluate local officials while facing scrutiny of their own response to failures. Publicity may therefore alter their personnel incentives even when official information is available. The paper extends research on selective accountability by connecting provincial news supervision to routine career decisions across failures with and without matched newspaper coverage.
